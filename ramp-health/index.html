Ramp Status Management System

* This case study focuses on the design process and the reasoning behind decisions. It contains no confidential details.

The Ramp Status Management System is a data monitoring and management platform for the production ramp of new products in Apple’s internal supply chain. It brings data from a dozen-plus modules, once scattered across email, Excel, offline documents and department systems, into one real-time ramp status dashboard that spans every product. Operations managers who own different modules collaborate live and report efficiently, leaders see alerts and risks at a glance, and data updates move from weekly to minute-level.

As the UI/UX designer, I led the system’s information architecture on my own in the early phase, along with the design of the Summary home page. Later I worked in parallel with a second designer to extend the design to every module, and supported engineering through implementation.

Ramp: a fast-paced, highly uncertain stage of the supply chain

01 · Background

Before every new Apple product goes on sale, it passes through the ramp: the window in which production moves from small pilot runs to full mass production. Output and yield climb step by step until they reach target capacity and the expected shipping capability.

An anomaly missed during the ramp = a potentially enormous cost

02 · Risk scenarios

Data scattered across more than six places

03 · The status quo

Before the system existed, the operations managers of each business module maintained their own key ramp metrics and reported upward in whatever way their module had always used. Leaders saw a static snapshot of one point in time, not a live overall view, which made it hard to grasp the whole picture early or to spot risks that cut across modules.

  • Excel files

    Data folded away in multiple, multi-level Excel files

  • Siloed data

    Each department managed its own database

  • PM-maintained data

    Some data and reporting methods lived only with individual PMs

  • Email floods

    Key issues and anomalies travelled by email: late, hard to trace, and flooding inboxes

  • Slow data-access requests

    Getting data across departments meant finding the right person and the right permission

  • Offline report decks

    Compiled by hand into 100+ page reports and presented upward every week

Show the data + support decisions.

04 · Design goals

The system is shared by many departments and roles, so the UX has to serve both the people who study the data in detail every day and the people who scan it quickly to make decisions.

User type A · Leadership

“How is each product’s ramp going? Where are the risks? What needs attention next?”

User type B · Day-to-day working teams

View the data for their module, flag anomalies quickly, predict upstream and downstream impact accurately, and report upward promptly.

One design language connecting different business modules

06 · Design & business

The goal was not to make every module use identical UI components. It was to keep the style consistent at system level while letting each business domain express its own data logic.

  • Line buildup

    Progress and status

    Progress bars and status charts show the planned number of lines, current buildup progress and the lines now in production.

  • Quality hold

    Impact scope and root-cause tracing

    A flow diagram visualizes which parts and stages a quality hold affects.

  • Key milestones

    Milestone dates and changes

    The supply chain’s other key milestones are organized around the launch dates, with a change history and reminders for key dates.

  • Key component data

    Dense data

    Repeated fields are merged, and data lists follow the levels of the business hierarchy.

07 · Design examples

Source systems date from different eras and look different

↓

A blended, unified design language for the system

The design language neither copies the UI of Apple’s desktop operating system nor reuses the style of the internal business systems: the first is too eye-catching, the second too dull. The final direction sits between the two. It keeps the components that recur across existing systems, which lowers the learning cost, and uses color, space and charts more freely, so users can tell information apart and locate it faster in large volumes of data.

Leadership learns about supply chain data through weekly reports

↓

The latest data, synced in one click and always available

Factory production status changes in real time, but the data used to be confirmed level by level and then compiled into report documents, so leaders’ view of anomalies and progress lagged behind. In the system, working-level users can see the live data synced from the factory at any time. Once they confirm it, that version of the data is synced to management users.

Business data was reported by data source, in document order

↓

Modules and features grouped by production and logistics phase

Ramp data is divided into three phases, following the way the factory organizes production. Unlike a linear report document, the system lets users cross-reference data between business modules whenever they need to.

From Wave 1 to Wave 2, still growing.

09 · Reflections

  1. Distill operations into interface

    Guided by the product managers, I first worked out the core information each business module needs to show, and which steps people repeat back and forth when they prepare a ramp report. Then I turned the key information into clear pages and the repeated steps into things the system does automatically.

  2. Build a shared example first, then work in parallel

    The project has more than ten sub-modules, so I started with the Summary page that every module needs. That won early agreement on the design style from all the module owners. After that I refined each sub-module according to its own needs.

  3. Balance data completeness and readability

    I confirmed the design with each module owner: keep the supply chain data in its original shape, and keep the interface clean and easy to read. The balance between the two keeps shifting.

  4. Design AI workflows that work for the business

    I simulated the tasks people do most often to find the screens where AI fits, and gave those moments one visual treatment. The features include AI-generated supporting text, drawn from historical data and other modules, when someone adds a remark; jumping from an anchor concept to related data in the right module; and a chatbot for overall questions about ramp status.

  5. Components evolve with business needs

    After I left the project, the design style for dense supply chain data and the rules for reusing components are still in use. Components are built and iterated alongside business needs, so new requirements keep landing quickly and the component library never drifts away from the business.
